Barley and Root Vegetable Porridge

Ingredients

  • 1 cup barley
  • 2 carrots, peeled and diced
  • 1 turnip, peeled and diced
  • 1 onion, finely chopped
  • 4 cups vegetable or meat broth
  • 1 tsp salt
  • 1 tsp dried thyme or wild herbs
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 2 tbsp butter or animal fat

Directions

  1. Rinse the barley under cold water, then soak for at least 30 minutes.
  2. In a large pot, melt the butter or animal fat over medium heat.
  3. Add the onion, carrots, and turnip. Sauté until softened, about 5 minutes.
  4. Drain the barley and add it to the pot. Stir to coat it in the fat.
  5. Add the broth, salt, thyme, and bay leaf. Bring to a boil.
  6. Lower the heat and simmer uncovered for about 45-50 minutes, stirring occasionally, until the barley is tender and the liquid is mostly absorbed.
  7. Remove the bay leaf, adjust seasoning, and serve hot!

A Little Bit of History...

Barley and root vegetables were staples in the Viking diet, especially during the colder months when fresh greens were scarce. Porridge made with grains like barley provided much-needed energy and could be easily adapted with whatever vegetables were on hand. The combination of barley, carrots, and turnips was common, as these root vegetables grew well in the rugged Scandinavian terrain.

Barley, in particular, was a key grain in Viking agriculture, used not only in porridges but also in bread and beer. Its hardy nature made it ideal for the tough, northern climates. Root vegetables such as turnips and carrots were easy to store through the winter, making them a reliable source of nutrition during the Vikings' long, dark winters. This simple yet filling porridge sustained Viking families and warriors alike, offering both warmth and sustenance.

Porridges were not only for daily meals but often made for communal feasts, where food was shared among Viking clans. The preparation of such dishes reflected the importance of community and survival. The addition of wild herbs, like thyme, added flavor and a connection to the land that the Vikings relied on. This dish, although humble, was a symbol of the Vikings' close relationship with nature and their ability to thrive in even the harshest conditions.
